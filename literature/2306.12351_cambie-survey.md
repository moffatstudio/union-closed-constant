## 0. Abstract (as given, unlabeled, opening paragraph)

> Mathematicians had little idea whether the easy-to-state union-closed conjecture was true or false even after $40$ years. However, last winter saw a surge of interest in the conjecture and its variants, initiated by the contribution of a researcher at Google. Justin Gilmer made a significant breakthrough by discovering a first constant lower bound for the proportion of the most common element in a union-closed family.

## 1. Introduction of the Union-Closed conjecture

Ground set $[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$. Subset $A\subseteq[n]$. Family $\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}$.

A family $\mathcal{F}$ is **union-closed** if for every $A,B\in\mathcal{F}$, $A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}$. Equivalently $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$, where $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F} := \{A\cup B\mid A,B\in\mathcal{F}\}$.

Example family $\mathcal{F}_m$ (for every $m \in \mathbb{N}$):
$$\mathcal{F}_{m}=\{A\mid A\subseteq[m]\vee A=[k]\text{ for some }m+1\leq k\leq m^{2}\}$$
— consists of the $2^m$ subsets of $[m]$, plus the $m^2-m$ intervals $[k]$ for $m+1 \le k \le m^2$.

### Conjecture 1 (Union-closed conjecture).
*If $\mathcal{F}\neq\{\emptyset\}$ is a union-closed family with ground set $[n]$, then there exists an element $i\in[n]$ such that at least half of the sets in $\mathcal{F}$ contain $i$.*

Remarks:
- Considering $\mathcal{F}_m$ for large $m$: only a small fraction of ground-set elements can be abundant (≥ half of sets), and their average proportion of membership can tend to $0$.
- The conjecture is (arguably) false on an infinite ground set $\mathbb{N}$, e.g. taking the union-closed family of all finite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$.
- Equivalent bitstring formulation: subsets of $\{0,1\}^n$ closed under element-wise OR.
- Taking complements gives the **Intersection-closed sets conjecture**: an intersection-closed family has an element appearing in **at most** half the sets. (Cites [3, Sec. 3] for lattice-, graph-, and Salzborn-formulations.)
- Nov 17, 2022: Justin Gilmer [10] (Google, ML researcher) proved the first constant-fraction lower bound for Conjecture 1. This triggered a wave of arXiv preprints within days.

The note's roadmap: Section 2 = Gilmer's approach; Sections 3–4 = extensions of his method; Section 5 = an unsuccessful attempt (Scandone); Section 6 = other related work.

## 2. The observations and key elements in the proof by Gilmer

Gilmer's first observation: prove the **contrapositive** of Conjecture 1 — "If a non-empty family $\mathcal{F}$ has no element appearing in at least half of the sets of $\mathcal{F}$, then $\mathcal{F}$ is not a union-closed family." Since $A\cup A = A$ for every set $A$, $\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$, so $\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert>\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$ whenever $\mathcal{F}$ is not union-closed.

Gilmer's key idea: use **entropy**. (Noted while studying counterexamples to variants of Conjecture 1 similar to those in [8].)

**Shannon entropy definition** given explicitly: for discrete r.v. $X$ with outcomes $x \in A$ (finite set), probability $p_x$:
$$H(X)=-\sum_{x\in A}p_{x}\log_{2}p_{x}.$$

Key entropy fact used: sampling uniformly at random from $\mathcal{F}$ gives entropy exactly $\log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$, the maximum possible over distributions supported on $\mathcal{F}$. **Core of Gilmer's approach**: if one can sample from $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$ (via some coupling of two $\mathcal{F}$-valued variables $A,B$) such that $H(A\cup B) > \log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$, then $\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert > \lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$.

### Theorem 2. (Gilmer's main entropy inequality)
*Let $A$ and $B$ denote independent and identically distributed random variables that sample from a common distribution over subsets of $[n]$. Assume that for all $i\in[n]$, $\mathbb{P}[i\in A]\leq 0.01$. Then*
$$H(A\cup B)\geq 1.26\,H(A).$$

**Corollary (stated, with footnote update — see below):** By taking the uniform distribution over the subsets of $[n]$: if $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ is a family for which every element is contained in no more than $1\%$ of the sets, then
$$\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert\geq\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert^{1.26}.$$

**Footnote 3 (attached to this corollary), verbatim content:** "As a corollary of later work by Sawin, this is at least $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert^{1.74}$." (I.e., Sawin's later work improves Gilmer's exponent $1.26$ to $1.74$ in this specific "every element ≤ 1% of sets" regime.)

Consequence stated in the main text (using the $1.26$/$0.01$ pairing as originally proved by Gilmer): whenever $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\geq 2$, either $\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert>\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$ (family not union-closed) or there is an element appearing in at least a $0.01$ fraction of the sets of $\mathcal{F}$. **Conclusion: Conjecture 1 is true with "half" replaced by $0.01$.**

(Historical note for context — not explicitly given as a separate number here beyond the above — Gilmer's original paper's headline constant for the general problem, discussed later, is characterized via the derivation below.)

### example 3.
$\mathcal{F}=\{\{1\},\{2\}\}$, so $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\}\}$. With $A,B$ i.i.d. uniform on $\mathcal{F}$:
$$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B=\{1\})=\mathbb{P}(A\cup B=\{2\})=\tfrac14,\qquad \mathbb{P}(A\cup B=\{1,2\})=\tfrac12.$$
$H(A)=2\cdot\tfrac12\log_2 2 = 1$; $H(A\cup B)=2\cdot\tfrac14\log_2 4+\tfrac12\log 2=\tfrac32\;(<\log_2 3)$.

Exact concluding sentence (verbatim): "Since $\log_2(2)<H(A\cup B)$, we conclude that it is impossible that $A\cup B$ takes values in a family with only $2$ elements and thus $\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert>\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$, i.e. Gilmer's method verifies that $\mathcal{F}$ is not union-closed."

### example 4. (An example where Gilmer's method fails to certify non-union-closedness, despite a fairly large abundant fraction)
$\mathcal{F}=\binom{[3]}{\leq 2}$ (all subsets of $[3]$ of size $\le 2$), so $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=2^{[3]}$. $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert=7$; every $i\in[3]$ appears in exactly $3$ sets, i.e., fraction $\frac37$.

With $A,B$ i.i.d. uniform on $\mathcal{F}$:
$$\mathbb{P}(A\cup B=\emptyset)=\tfrac1{49},\quad \mathbb{P}(\lvert A\cup B\rvert=1)=\tfrac3{49},\quad \mathbb{P}(\lvert A\cup B\rvert=2)=\tfrac9{49},\quad \mathbb{P}(A\cup B=[3])=\tfrac{12}{49}.$$
$$H(A)=7\cdot\tfrac17\log_2(7)=\log_2(7)\sim 2.81.$$
$$H(A\cup B)=\tfrac1{49}\log_2(49)+3\cdot\tfrac3{49}\log_2(49/3)+3\cdot\tfrac9{49}\log_2(49/9)+\tfrac{12}{49}\log_2(49/12)\sim 2.70.$$
Thus $H(A) > H(A\cup B)$: **Gilmer's method (with the i.i.d.-uniform coupling) does not certify that $\mathcal{F}$ is not union-closed, even though the maximum element-occurrence fraction is $\frac37$** — illustrating a limitation of the naive entropy approach even on small/simple families.

**Note (analogous, larger example):** For $\mathcal{F}=\binom{[5]}{\leq 3}$: $H(A)=\log_2(26)\sim 4.7$ and $H(A\cup B)\sim 4.54$; every element appears in a $\frac{11}{26}$ fraction.

## 3. Quick refinement of Gilmer's idea

**Binary entropy function:** $h(p) = -(p\log_2 p + (1-p)\log_2(1-p))$.

Key fact used by Gilmer: $h(p)\leq h(2p-p^2)$ whenever $p \leq \psi := \dfrac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$.

Gilmer claimed his ideas extend to prove a fraction equal to $\psi$ (numerically, $\psi = \frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx 0.381966\ldots$ — this is the golden-ratio-related constant $1 - 1/\varphi = 2 - \varphi$, i.e. $\psi = \varphi^{-2}$ where $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$).

Four papers "quickly implemented this approach" — **[1] Alweiss–Huang–Sellke, [5] Chase–Lovett, [18] Sawin, [15] Pebody** — and reduced Conjecture 1 for the constant $\psi$ to the following key lemma:

### Lemma 5.
*Let $\phi=\dfrac{\sqrt5+1}{2}$ and $0\leq x\leq 1$. Then*
$$h(x^{2})\geq \phi\, x\, h(x).$$

- Proved two different ways: **[1]** Alweiss–Huang–Sellke via accurate computer calculations + interval arithmetic on three intervals; **[18]** Sawin via a purely calculus-based approach. (Communication between authors of [1] and [5] led to [5] citing [1]'s formal proof.) **[15]** Pebody split the lemma into two parts without formal proof, but both parts "can be verified easily."
- **A short and more elegant proof was given later by Boppana [2]**, even though the proof "would originate from 1989." Relies on:

### Theorem 6. (extension of the classical Rolle's theorem; follows from observations in e.g. [12] Kostov)
*Let $f$ be a differentiable function on an interval $I$. Let $m(f)$ be the sum of multiplicities of the roots of $f$ in $I$. Then*
$$m(f')\geq m(f)-1.$$

By iterating three times: $m(f) \leq m(f''') + 3$. Applying this to $f(x) = h(x^2) - \phi x h(x)$ and counting multiplicities of the roots $0, \frac1\phi, 1$ of $f$, one quickly concludes $f \geq 0$ on $[0,1]$ — this is exactly Lemma 5.

**Once Lemma 5 is derived, the proof of Conjecture 1 for constant $\psi$ (instead of $0.5$) is short** in each of [1,5,15,18] — noted as illustrated by the short total length of the Chase–Lovett paper [5]. Chase–Lovett's proof has three steps:
1. Extend the analytic claim (Lemma 5) to the **two-variate function**
$$f(x,y):=\frac{h(xy)}{h(x)y+h(y)x}.$$
2. Prove a strengthened inequality between $H(A\cup B)$ and $H(A), H(B)$ for (not-necessarily-identical) random variables $A,B$ on $\{0,1\}^n$ where every bit is $1$ with bounded probability.
3. Extend to **approximate union-closed families** — families where the union of two randomly drawn sets belongs to the family with high probability.

### Sharpness example for the $\psi$ bound
Family: $\mathcal{F}_1+\mathcal{F}_2 := \{A \mid A\in\mathcal{F}_1 \vee A\in\mathcal{F}_2\}$ where
$$\mathcal{F}_{1}=\binom{[n]}{\psi n+n^{2/3}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}_{2}=\binom{[n]}{\geq(1-\psi)n}.$$
Here $\lvert\mathcal{F}_1\rvert \gg \lvert\mathcal{F}_2\rvert$, and the union of two i.i.d. uniform random sets from $\mathcal{F}_1$ lies in $\mathcal{F}_2$ with very high probability: the expected size of the union is $n-(1-\psi)^2 n + \Theta(n^{2/3}) = (1-\psi)n + \Theta(n^{2/3})$ (slightly larger than $(1-\psi)n$ by an order-$n^{2/3}$ term), and since the variance of the size is $O(n^{1/2})$, the union almost surely belongs to $\mathcal{F}_2$. The conclusion holds for any $g(n)$ with $n \gg g(n) \gg n^{1/2}$ in place of $n^{2/3}$.

**"Figure 2: An approximate union-closed family whose elements appear in at most a $\psi+o(1)$ fraction."** — This is the extremal/near-tight example showing the entropy-method bound of $\psi \approx 0.38197$ cannot trivially be beaten by this construction-type argument for *approximate* union-closed families.

**Note:** In his original paper Gilmer included ideas toward a full resolution of Conjecture 1, but "some of these directions were immediately proven not to hold by Sawin and Ellis [18, 7]."

## 4. Further refinements and extensions related to Gilmer's work — THE COUPLING FRAMEWORK AND THE BARRIER

This section is the core of items (b) and (c) of the requested extraction.

> "Sawin [18] gave a suggestion to improve the bound further, which given the sharpness of the form for union-closed families may be considered surprising. Hereby the essence is in a question purely stated in terms of probability distributions."
>
> "His suggestion was worked out by Yu [20] and Cambie [4]."

### Yu's dimension-free approach [20]
- Yu [20] "considered the approach in a slightly more general form initially and made a lower bound computable by restricting to the suggestion of Sawin and applying **[1, Lem. 5]** and the **Krein–Milman theorem [13]** to bound the support (number of values with nonzero probability) of a joint distribution by $4$."
- **"A numerical computation then yield a bound equal to (roughly) $0.38234$."**

This is the explicit statement of the 0.38234 numerical record from the coupling/Sawin-suggestion approach, obtained by Yu via: (i) reformulating Sawin's suggestion as an optimization over probability distributions, (ii) using the Krein–Milman theorem to reduce to distributions supported on at most $4$ points (extreme points of the relevant convex set / feasible region), (iii) applying Lemma 5 of [1], and (iv) solving numerically.

### Cambie's barrier / matching upper bound [4] — THE BARRIER DISCUSSION
> "In parallel, Cambie [4] found an upper bound for Sawin's approach which indicates that the improvement is way smaller than expected and one would hope for. The construction is a discrete probability distribution with only two values having nonzero probability, with the values determined by a system of equations involving the entropy function."
>
> "Additionally he proved that this value is sharp, by first reducing the support to $3$ elements, where one of the elements equals $1$. Finally, the conclusion is derived from the combination of $3$-dimensional plots, a numerical minimization problem and a more precise solution for the case where the support has exactly two elements, one of which equals $1$."

**Exact structure of the barrier argument as summarized here:**
1. Cambie constructs an explicit **upper bound** for what Sawin's suggested coupling-based approach can possibly achieve — i.e., a matching construction showing the method cannot be pushed meaningfully beyond Yu's $\approx 0.38234$ figure.
2. The extremal/worst-case construction for the upper bound is a **discrete probability distribution supported on exactly two values** (nonzero probability at two points), with those values pinned down by solving a system of equations involving the entropy function $h$ (i.e., an optimality/critical-point system, not a closed form).
3. Cambie proves this two-point-support upper bound value is **sharp** (matches what the coupling approach can achieve) via a proof strategy that:
   - first reduces the general problem (arbitrary support) down to support size **3**, where one of the three support elements is fixed to equal $1$;
   - then finishes by combining **3-dimensional plots** (visual/numerical exploration of the 3-variable reduced problem), a **numerical minimization problem**, and a more precise (presumably closed-form or rigorously bounded) analysis of the **2-element-support sub-case** (again with one point equal to $1$).

**Interpretation stated explicitly by Cambie in the concluding section (Section 6) — this is the paper's own barrier commentary:**
> "Taking into account that the improvement by taking combinations suggested by Sawin [18] turned out to be tinier than expected and hoped for, as illustrated by the example in [4], it seems that the focus should go towards essential new ideas."

This is the survey's explicit statement of *why* the entropy/coupling method (as extended via Sawin's suggestion, computed by Yu, and matched from above by Cambie) is **barrier-limited**: Cambie's matching upper-bound construction shows the achievable improvement over $\psi \approx 0.38197$ (the "quick refinement" constant from Section 3) via this whole coupling framework is minuscule — landing at $\approx 0.38234$ — and cannot be substantially pushed further using this style of argument (extreme-point / support-reduction analysis of Sawin's coupling-distribution optimization problem). The extremal examples that pin down both the lower bound (Yu's numerical optimum over ≤4-point-support distributions) and the upper bound (Cambie's 2-point/3-point-support constructions) are what "blocks" further improvement: the method's own sharp examples cap it near $0.38234$.

### Yuster's almost-$k$-union-closed generalization [21]
Building on Chase–Lovett [5]'s approximate-union-closed framework, Yuster [21] considers families that are **almost $k$-union-closed**: the union of $k$ independent uniform random sets from $\mathcal{F}$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}$ with high probability.

He conjectures a **tight version for the minimum frequency** of some element in such families, with threshold $\psi_k$ := the unique real root in $[0,1]$ of
$$(1-x)^{k}=x.$$

Sharpness/intuition example (generalizing the Section 3 construction): union of $\mathcal{F}_1 = \binom{[n]}{\psi_k n + n^{2/3}}$ and $\mathcal{F}_2 = \binom{[n]}{\geq (1-\psi_k)n}$. If at least one of the $k$ drawn sets is from $\mathcal{F}_2$, the union is guaranteed to be in $\mathcal{F}_2$. If all $k$ sets are from $\mathcal{F}_1$, expected union size is $n - (1-\psi_k)^k n + \Theta(n^{2/3})$, variance $O(n^{1/2})$, so again the union almost surely lands in $\mathcal{F}_2$.

**"The conjecture is proven to be true for $k \leq 4$, while for larger values of $k$ a weaker bound is established."**

(Note: for $k=2$, $\psi_k = \psi$, recovering the Gilmer/Section-3 constant.)

## 5. The final Eureka moment, not yet (Scandone's flawed attempted full proof)

Scandone [19] uploaded a preprint claiming a full resolution of Conjecture 1; excitement followed, but the proposed solution had "several issues, including a significant flaw that requires revising the underlying construction" — **communicated to Scandone by Terence Tao**.

Cambie explains Scandone's idea (deemed to hold "potential" and valuable intuition) in detail:

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be not union-closed, so $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\neq\mathcal{F}$. A r.v. valued in $\mathcal{F}$ has entropy $\le \log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$, equality iff uniform. By example (e.g. $\mathcal{F}=\{\{1\},\{2\}\}$) there is *no* strategy for $A,B$ sampling $\mathcal{F}$ such that $A\cup B$ samples uniformly from $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$. But: if for every $A\in\mathcal{F}$ the probability of obtaining it stays almost equal to the original, while a few sets in $(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathcal{F}$ get small probability, entropy can still increase — because $h'$ is continuously decreasing on $(0,1)$ with $h'(0)=+\infty$ (Figure 3 plots $h$).

**Scandone's construction:** Let $A,B$ i.i.d. uniform on $\mathcal{F}$. Define $\mathcal{P}([n])$-valued r.v. $A^\delta$ via
$$\Pr[A^{\delta}=X]=(1-\delta)\Pr[A=X]+\delta\Pr[A\cup B=X]\quad\text{for every }X\subseteq[n].$$

For $X\in\mathcal{F}$: $\Pr[A^\delta = X] \geq (1-\delta)\Pr[A=X]$, so for $\delta$ small,
$$h(\Pr[A^{\delta}=X])-h(\Pr[A=X])\gtrsim \delta/\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\; h'(1/\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert).$$
**Footnote 4 (precise condition, verbatim):** "To be precise, we assume $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\geq 3$ and $\dfrac{2}{\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}+\delta<1$."

For $X \in (\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathcal{F}$, with $p := \Pr[A\cup B = X]$: $h(\delta p)\sim -\delta p(\log\delta+\log p-1)$. Choosing $\delta$ small enough that $-\log\delta \gg \frac1p h'(1/\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert)$ ensures $H(A^\delta) > H(A)$.

Equivalently, $A^\delta$ arises from $A,B$ plus an independent Bernoulli($\delta$) variable $Z_\delta$ choosing between $A\cup B$ and $A$.

**The flaw (as communicated by Tao), verbatim explanation given:**
> "The flaw in the argument is that, in the process of revealing all the digits of $A^{\delta}$ (computed using the chain rule for the entropy), the indeterminacy provided by $Z_{\delta}$ (and the consequent improvement of the bounds) is lost after the first step. More precisely, there is [a] step in the computations in which a conditional probability distribution has been erroneously replaced by its expected value, and this produces the aforementioned flaw in the argument."
>
> Tao's comment, as rephrased in the survey: **"the idea of modifying the union operation by Gilmer is promising, but a single global bit $Z_\delta$ is not sufficient to do the job, and a more involved construction is needed."**

## 6. A better understanding by progress in a different direction

Covers three works giving other angles on Conjecture 1 (brief per instructions, but transcribed in full for completeness):

### Cui–Hu's stronger conjecture and its refutation
**Conjecture 7** (Cui and Hu [6]; would imply Conjecture 1):
*If $\mathcal{F}$ is a finite union-closed family of sets whose smallest set is of size at least $2$, then there are at least two elements such that each belongs to more than half of the sets of $\mathcal{F}$.*

Kabela, Polák, Teska [11] (end of 2022) proved Conjecture 7 is **false** when "$2$" is replaced by a larger integer: there exist families all of whose sets have size $\geq k$ (for arbitrarily large $k$) with only $2$ abundant elements. Main construction: family $\mathcal{P}^{12}_4$ = all subsets $S \subseteq \{0,1,\ldots,11\}$ of size $\geq 4$ such that either $\{0,1\}\subset S$, or ($0\in S$ and $S \subseteq \{0,2,4,\ldots,10\}$), or ($1 \in S$ and $S \subseteq \{1,3,5,\ldots,11\}$). Verified: $\lvert\mathcal{P}^{12}_4\rvert = (2^{10}-11)+2\cdot16 = 1045$; every element $2 \le i \le 11$ appears $2^9 - 1 + 11 = 522$ times.

Poonen [16] "block" definition (maximal set of elements belonging to exactly the same sets of a family); to prove Conjecture 1 it suffices to focus on families with no singleton blocks. Cambie notes the $\mathcal{P}^{12}_4$ construction generalizes to such block-free families: for fixed $k \geq 3$ and sufficiently large even $n$ (e.g. $n \geq 10k$), with $E_n, O_n$ the even/odd elements of $[n]$, the family $\mathcal{P}^n_k$ of subsets $S \subseteq [n]$ with $\lvert S\rvert \geq k$ and either $\{1,2\}\subset S$, or ($S \subseteq E_n$ and $2\in S$), or ($S \subseteq O_n$ and $1 \in S$) — has $1,2$ abundant and (for $n$ large) all other elements non-abundant.

### Ellis–Ivan–Leader [9] and Pulaj–Wood [17]
Ellis, Ivan, Leader (early 2023) [9] proved: for every $k \in \mathbb{N}$ there is a union-closed family whose (unique) smallest set has size $k$, but every element of that smallest set has frequency $(1+o(1))\frac{\log k}{2k}$ — i.e., **focusing on the smallest set's elements cannot work, in the strongest possible sense.** They also posed an open problem verifying the conjecture for a specific family they could not resolve; this was **verified by Pulaj and Wood [17]**, who also proved new bounds on the least $m = m(k,n)$ such that every union-closed family $\mathcal{F}$ containing some $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \binom{[n]}{k}$ with $\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert = m$ as a subfamily satisfies Conjecture 1.

## Concluding remarks / OPEN QUESTIONS (as stated in the survey)

The survey's final paragraph (Section 6, end) is the source of its explicit open-problem framing:

> "We can conclude that despite the progress that originates from the breakthrough of Justin Gilmer, the exact version of Conjecture 1 is still not proven. Mathematicians are still thinking about other directions or modifications of the strategy and hope to resolve Conjecture 1 in the future. Taking into account that the improvement by taking combinations suggested by Sawin [18] turned out to be tinier than expected and hoped for, as illustrated by the example in [4], it seems that the focus should go towards essential new ideas."

**The explicit open generalization stated (with footnote attributing the remark):**
> "In particular, the union-closed conjecture might be a distraction of a more general behaviour that
> $$\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert>\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert^{c}$$
> for some $c(\varepsilon)>1$ when every element of $[n]$ appears in less than a $\frac12-\varepsilon$ fraction of the sets in $\mathcal{F}$."

**Footnote 5 (attribution, verbatim):** "communicated by Zachary Chase"

**"Note added"** (post-submission update, dated within the note): *"In June 2023, Liu [14] improved the constant slightly with a different method of coupling."* — This refers to J. Liu, "Improving the Lower Bound for the Union-closed Sets Conjecture via Conditionally IID Coupling," arXiv:2306.08824 (June 2023). No numeric value for Liu's constant is given in this survey text itself (only the qualitative note that it is a further, different, coupling-based improvement beyond the Yu/Cambie $0.38234$-type figure).

### Enumerated / itemizable open sub-questions found in the survey (there is no formally numbered "Open Problem" list in this paper — it is a narrative survey — but the following are the discrete open questions/directions it explicitly raises or leaves open):
1. **The general "$c(\varepsilon)$" question** (end of Section 6, attributed to Zachary Chase): is it true that $\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert>\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert^{c}$ for some $c(\varepsilon)>1$ whenever every element of $[n]$ appears in less than a $\frac12-\varepsilon$ fraction of the sets of $\mathcal{F}$? (I.e., a dimension/parameter-free strengthening — the suggestion that the whole union-closed phenomenon is a special case of an entropy-supermultiplicativity statement parametrized by $\varepsilon$.)
2. **Push past the coupling-method barrier ($\approx 0.38234$)**: the survey frames this implicitly as closed off for Sawin's specific coupling suggestion (Cambie's matching upper bound shows the gain is "tinier than expected and hoped for"), and states the conclusion that **"the focus should go towards essential new ideas"** rather than further refining this particular coupling approach.
3. **Yuster's conjecture [21]** on the tight minimum frequency threshold $\psi_k$ (root of $(1-x)^k=x$) for almost-$k$-union-closed families — proved only for $k \leq 4$; open (with only a weaker bound known) for $k > 4$.
4. **Repairing Scandone's approach**: Tao's comment leaves open whether some more involved (multi-bit / non-global) randomized construction along the lines of Scandone's idea could succeed where the single global Bernoulli bit $Z_\delta$ fails — i.e., whether Gilmer's "modify the union operation" idea can be rescued by more sophisticated indeterminacy.
5. **Cui–Hu-style stronger conjectures** — Conjecture 7 itself remains of interest for the "smallest set size ≥ 2 ⟹ two abundant elements" case (only *larger* minimum-set-size thresholds were refuted by Kabela–Polák–Teska); the boundary of exactly which smallest-set-size guarantees which number of abundant elements is not fully resolved in this note.
6. The overall union-closed sets conjecture (Conjecture 1) itself remains **open** at the constant $\frac12$; best proven constant via this whole entropy/coupling program as of the note (pre-Liu) is Yu's $\approx 0.38234$ (with Cambie's construction indicating this coupling-refinement route is nearly exhausted), and Liu's June 2023 paper is flagged as a further (unspecified-in-this-note) improvement via a different coupling method.

| Alweiss–Huang–Sellke [1], Chase–Lovett [5], Sawin [18], Pebody [15] | Conjecture 1 true for constant $\psi=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx 0.38197$ (in place of $0.5$) | Lemma 5 ($h(x^2)\ge \phi x h(x)$, $\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$) via extended Rolle's theorem (Boppana's proof) |
| Yu [20] | numerical bound $\approx 0.38234$ | Sawin's coupling suggestion, support ≤ 4 via Krein–Milman + Lemma 5 of [1], solved numerically |
| Cambie [4] | matching upper bound showing the true optimum of Sawin's coupling approach is only marginally above $\psi$ — the $\approx0.38234$-type gain is essentially the ceiling of this method | 2-point-support construction (entropy-function equation system) + sharpness proof (reduce to 3-point support with one point $=1$; 3D plots + numerical minimization + exact 2-point-support analysis) |
| Yuster [21] | almost-$k$-union-closed threshold $\psi_k$ = root of $(1-x)^k = x$ in $[0,1]$, proved for $k \le 4$ | extension of Chase–Lovett's approximate-union-closed machinery |
| Liu [14] (June 2023, "Note added") | "improved the constant slightly" (no value given in this survey) | "different method of coupling" (conditionally i.i.d. coupling) |
| Kabela–Polák–Teska [11] | refutes Cui–Hu Conjecture 7 for smallest-set-size thresholds larger than 2 (constructions with only 2 abundant elements for arbitrarily large minimum set size $k$) | explicit combinatorial construction $\mathcal{P}^{12}_4$, generalized to $\mathcal{P}^n_k$ |
