**Practical consequence:** AHS, Chase–Lovett, and Pebody all phrase their core one-variable inequality in the *flipped* variable $s=1-p$ (i.e. $s$ close to $1$ means the *element is rarely present*), while Gilmer and Sawin phrase theirs directly in $p$ (probability the element *is* present). Boppana's $x$ is also the flipped variable $s$. The single golden-ratio point that is extremal *everywhere* in this literature is:
$$p=\tau=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ s=1-p=\sigma=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}=1/\Phi.$$

## 1. The combinatorial statement being attacked

**Frankl's conjecture.** If $\mathcal{F}\subseteq2^{[n]}$ is union-closed ($A,B\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}$) and $\mathcal{F}\ne\{\emptyset\}$, then some $i\in[n]$ lies in at least half the sets of $\mathcal{F}$.

**What is actually proved (all six papers combined):** some $i\in[n]$ lies in at least a $\tau=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx0.382$ fraction of the sets of $\mathcal{F}$, and $\tau$ is the exact value beyond which the entropy method (in its current, "coordinate-marginal-only" form — see §4) provably cannot go, both for the exact conjecture (AHS's Theorem 1 + Claim 3/4) and even for a relaxed *approximate* version of it (Chase–Lovett's Theorem 1.3 + Example 1.4).

## 2. The entropy method, in one notation

**Setup.** Let $\mathcal{F}\subseteq2^{[n]}$ be union-closed, $\mathcal{F}\ne\{\emptyset\}$. Let $A,B$ be independent, uniformly random samples from $\mathcal{F}$. Write $A_i=\mathbb{1}[i\in A]$, $A_{<i}=(A_1,\dots,A_{i-1})$ (and similarly $B$). All entropies base 2, $H(A)=\log_2|\mathcal{F}|$.

**Step 1 — Union-closure gives an upper bound on $H(A\cup B)$.** Because $\mathcal{F}$ is union-closed, $A\cup B$ is itself a (non-uniform, in general) distribution supported on $\mathcal{F}$. Since the uniform distribution maximizes entropy over any fixed finite support,
$$H(A\cup B)\ \le\ H(A)=\log_2|\mathcal{F}|. \tag{UB}$$
*(This is the only place exact union-closure is used. In Chase–Lovett's approximate version, (UB) degrades to $H(A\cup B)\le(1+O(\varepsilon))\log_2|\mathcal{F}|+O(\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon))$ — see §5.)*

**Step 2 — Chain rule + data processing reduces $H(A\cup B)$ to a per-coordinate statement.** By the entropy chain rule,
$$H(A\cup B)=\sum_{i=1}^n H\big((A\cup B)_i\mid(A\cup B)_{<i}\big),\qquad H(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n H(A_i\mid A_{<i}).$$
Since $(A\cup B)_{<i}$ is a deterministic function of $(A_{<i},B_{<i})$, conditioning on strictly more information cannot increase entropy less than conditioning on the function of it (data-processing / the "more information $\Rightarrow$ no more entropy" fact, proved from $I(X:f(Y))\le I(X:Y)$ in Gilmer §2):
$$H\big((A\cup B)_i\mid(A\cup B)_{<i}\big)\ \ge\ H\big((A\cup B)_i\mid A_{<i},B_{<i}\big). \tag{DP}$$
Write $p_i:=\Pr[i\in A\mid A_{<i}]$, $q_i:=\Pr[i\in B\mid B_{<i}]$ — random variables (functions of the histories) that are **i.i.d.** across the randomness of $A_{<i},B_{<i}$ (since $A,B$ are i.i.d. processes), with $\mathbb{E}[p_i]=\Pr[i\in A]\le u$ where $u:=\max_j\Pr[j\in A]$. Given the histories, $\Pr[i\in A\cup B]=p_i+q_i-p_iq_i$, so
$$H\big((A\cup B)_i\mid A_{<i},B_{<i}\big)=\mathbb{E}[H(p_i+q_i-p_iq_i)], \qquad H(A_i\mid A_{<i})=\mathbb{E}[H(p_i)].$$

**Step 3 — The one-variable reduction, $\Lambda(u)$.** Define, for $u\in[0,1]$,
$$\Lambda(u)\ :=\ \inf\Big\{\ \tfrac{\mathbb{E}_{p,q\sim\mu\times\mu}[H(p+q-pq)]}{\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mu}[H(p)]}\ :\ \mu\text{ a probability measure on }[0,1],\ \mathbb{E}_\mu[p]\le u\ \Big\}.$$
(This is the object all five entropy-method papers compute exactly; it is Sawin's $\lambda(u)$ divided into the two cases, and is what AHS's $F(\mu)\ge0$ / Pebody's Lemma 3–4 / Chase–Lovett's Claim 4.1 all ultimately pin down.) Combining (DP) with Step 3's per-coordinate bound $\mathbb{E}[H(p_i+q_i-p_iq_i)]\ge\Lambda(u)\,\mathbb{E}[H(p_i)]$ and summing over $i$ (chain rule again):
$$H(A\cup B)\ \ge\ \Lambda(u)\cdot H(A). \tag{LB}$$

**Step 4 — Combine (UB) and (LB).** $\Lambda(u)\cdot H(A)\le H(A\cup B)\le H(A)$, and $H(A)=\log_2|\mathcal{F}|>0$ (as $\mathcal{F}\ne\{\emptyset\}$), so **$\Lambda(u)\le1$**. The whole content of the papers is computing $\Lambda(u)$ exactly and finding the smallest $u$ for which $\Lambda(u)\le1$ is even possible — that $u$ is exactly $\tau$:
$$\Lambda(u)\ =\ \begin{cases}\dfrac{H(2u-u^2)}{H(u)} & u\le\tau\\[4pt] (1-u)\dfrac{2}{\sqrt5-1} & u\ge\tau\end{cases}, \qquad \Lambda(u)>1 \text{ for } u<\tau,\quad \Lambda(\tau)=1,\quad \Lambda(u)<1 \text{ for } u>\tau.$$
(Sawin Theorem 2/Lemma 3, stated exactly this way; equivalent content in AHS Theorem 2 + Corollary 9/10, Pebody Theorem 1, Chase–Lovett Claim 4.1.) Hence $\Lambda(u)\le1 \Rightarrow u\ge\tau$: **some element has marginal probability $\ge\tau$**, i.e. is contained in a $\ge\tau$ fraction of $\mathcal{F}$.

## 3. The exact one-variable inequality everything reduces to

**The single hardest fact, in each paper's language, is equivalent to:**
$$h(s^2)\ \ge\ \Phi\cdot s\cdot h(s)\qquad\text{for all }s\in[0,1],\ \ \Phi=\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\ \text{(golden ratio)}, \tag{$\star$}$$
**with equality exactly at $s\in\{0,\ \sigma,\ 1\}$, where $\sigma=1/\Phi=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}$** (double root at $0$ and at $\sigma$, simple root at $1$). This is **Boppana's Lemma**, verbatim.

Equivalent forms found in the other five papers (all provably the same statement under $s\leftrightarrow1-p$ or $s\leftrightarrow x$ as in §0's table):
- **AHS Claim 3:** for $x\in[\sigma,1]$, $\sigma H(x^2)\ge xH(x)$, equality iff $x\in\{\sigma,1\}$ — i.e. ($\star$) divided by $\Phi$ and restricted to $x\ge\sigma$ (the region $x<\sigma$ is not needed there because AHS's reduction, Theorem 1, already guarantees the extremal two-point measure has its nonzero atom at $x\ge\phi\ge\sigma$).
- **Chase–Lovett Claim 2.1:** $h(x^2)/(xh(x))$ minimized at $x=\sigma$ — the ratio form of ($\star$).
- **Sawin Lemma 7+8:** $H(s^2)<2sH(s)$ (a weak corollary of $\star$, since $\Phi<2$) plus $H(s^2)/(sH(s))$ decreasing then increasing around $\sigma$ — the same monotonicity content as ($\star$), proved by a *more* complicated route (needing a separate global-extremum argument) than Boppana's direct 5-root count.
- **Pebody Lemmas 5–6:** $H(x^2)/H(x)$ increasing on $[0,1]$, and $H(x^2)/(xH(x))$ increasing on $[\sigma,1]$ — again equivalent content, left **unproved** ("to be proven") in Pebody's own paper.

**Boppana's proof of ($\star$) — the cleanest, and historically the last of the six to appear (Jan 2023):** extend $h$ to $\mathbb{R}$, set $f(s):=h(s^2)-\Phi sh(s)$. Its third derivative is $f'''(s)=p(s)/[s(1-s^2)^2]$ for a cubic $p(s)=-\Phi s^3-4s^2+3\Phi s+2\Phi-4$ with negative leading coefficient and $p(0)<0$, hence at most one negative root, hence at most **two roots of $p$ in $(0,1)$**. By Rolle's theorem applied three times, $f$ has at most **five roots in $[0,1]$** counting multiplicity. Exhibiting five directly — double at $0$, double at $\sigma$, single at $1$ — accounts for *all* of them, so $f$ cannot change sign anywhere else on $[0,1]$; checking $f>0$ near $0^+$ finishes it. This is a **complete elementary replacement** for: AHS's computer-assisted numerical verification (their Appendix A, three sub-intervals, Lipschitz bounds, and two tables of $10^{-4}$-precision evaluations), Sawin's more roundabout Lemma 8 argument (which needed to *first* independently know $\sigma$ is a global minimum before the Rolle's-theorem step could run), and Pebody's admitted gap.

## 4. Why $\tau=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$ is a hard, structural barrier for this method

**It is not merely "the best bound anyone has found" — it is the provable exact value of $\sup\{u:\Lambda(u)>1\}$, and this supremum is witnessed by an actual concrete example, so no tightening of any single step above can do better.**

1. **The reduction $\Lambda(u)$ (Step 3) is provably tight as an optimization problem.** AHS Theorem 1 (via concavity of $F(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu\times\mu}H(xy)-\mathbb{E}_\mu H(x)$, Krein–Milman) and, independently, Pebody's Theorem 10/Corollary 11 (iterative atom-merging, Lemma 9) and Sawin's calculus-of-variations argument (his Lemma 3 proof, Steps 1–4) all prove — by *three structurally different arguments* reaching the same answer — that the infimum defining $\Lambda(u)$ is **attained**, and attained at a measure supported on **at most two points, one of which is a boundary point ($0$ or $1$ depending on convention)**. This collapses an infinite-dimensional optimization (over all probability measures $\mu$ on $[0,1]$ with mean $\le u$) to a **one-real-variable** calculus problem, which ($\star$) then solves exactly. Three independent proofs of the same reduction-to-two-points fact is strong evidence there is no slack left in Step 3 itself.

2. **The extremal distribution is realized by an actual, simple, explicit "family."** Gilmer's **Examples 1–2** (independent-coordinate Bernoulli($p$) sets, or "all-or-nothing" sets) achieve $H(A\cup B)/H(A)=H(2p-p^2)/H(p)$ *exactly*, i.e. **at $p=\tau$ this ratio equals exactly $1$** — matching Sawin's **Example 4** (independent-coordinate construction, sharp for $u\le\tau$) and **Example 5** (a mixture construction, sharp for $u\ge\tau$, using the identity $H(\tau)=H(\sigma)$ from complementarity $\tau+\sigma=1$). So the inequality $\Lambda(u)\le1\Rightarrow u\ge\tau$ (Step 4) is **tight as stated**: there is a genuine probability distribution over subsets — not merely an abstract worst case — realizing $\Lambda(u)=1$ exactly at $u=\tau$. Consequently **no strengthening of (DP), (LB), or the one-variable inequality ($\star$) alone can push the bound past $\tau$**, because doing so would have to falsely improve a genuinely tight inequality.

3. **Where the actual slack was hiding, and why it doesn't help (yet).** Two natural strengthenings were tried and explicitly **fail**:
 - **Gilmer's Conjecture 1** (use the KL-divergence identity $D(A\cup B\|A)+H(A\cup B)=H(A)$, valid when $A$ is exactly uniform on a union-closed $\mathcal{F}$, to squeeze out more than (UB) alone gives). **Disproved** by Sawin (Proposition 6, explicit construction with $H(A)$ linear in $n$ but $D(A\cup B\|A)=O(1)$) and independently by Ellis. The mechanism: the counterexample distribution is *not itself* required to come from an actual union-closed family — it is built purely at the level of "any distribution respecting the marginal-probability constraint," exactly like the entropy method's own reduction operates. So this route dies for the same structural reason the method is capped at $\tau$: an adversarial single/few-parameter family (here, a geometric mixture over nested "levels") saturates it.
 - **Sawin's own proposed fix (Section 2 "Proof Sketch," incomplete):** replace independence of $A,B$ with a *correlated* third sample $C$ (also uniform on $\mathcal{F}$, but coupled to $A$ to maximize $H(\max(A_i,C_i))$ at each coordinate), and take a convex combination $(1-\alpha)H(A\cup B)+\alpha H(A\cup C)$. This is explicitly **not** subject to the same tightness obstruction, because it breaks the "$A,B$ always independent" assumption baked into $\Lambda(u)$'s definition — but the sketch gives **no explicit $\delta>0$**, only an existence argument valid for $\alpha,\delta$ "sufficiently small." Whether this actually yields a numeric improvement past $\tau$ was left open in Sawin's paper (subsequent work by Cambie, arXiv:2212.12500, and Yu, arXiv:2212.00658, pursued this).
 - **Sawin's Question 9** crystallizes the diagnosis precisely: *drop the independence requirement between the two identically-distributed copies $A,B$ entirely* (keep only: same marginal law $\mu$, $\max_i\mu(i\in\cdot)<1/2$, $H>0$) — ask whether $H(A\cup B)>H(A)$ can still be forced for *some* coupling of $(A,B)$. A "yes" would resolve Frankl's conjecture outright, and by design **is not** subject to $\Lambda(u)$'s tightness barrier, since $\Lambda(u)$ is defined assuming independence.

**Summary diagnosis:** the entropy method's ceiling at $\tau$ comes from Step 2's data-processing move (DP), which **discards all information except the per-coordinate marginal probabilities $p_i,q_i$ conditioned on history** — in particular it discards any *correlation* between $A$ and $B$ (they are independent by construction) and any joint/global combinatorial structure that exact union-closure of $\mathcal{F}$ might otherwise impose across coordinates simultaneously. Once only marginals survive, the resulting one-variable problem $\Lambda(u)$ is a clean calculus problem with a known, exactly-attained extremum at $\tau$ — provably so, by three independent tightness proofs (AHS/Pebody/Sawin) plus an explicit witnessing example (Gilmer/Sawin). Beating $\tau$ requires re-injecting some of the discarded structure (correlation across $A,B$, or genuinely combinatorial facts about exact closure beyond "the union is usually back in $\mathcal{F}$") — exactly the two directions Sawin's Section 2 and Question 9 point toward, neither of which is completed in this literature.

## 5. Why $\tau$ is *also* the exact barrier for the approximate conjecture (Chase–Lovett), and which step needs only approximate closure

**Chase–Lovett's Theorem 1.3:** a $(1-\varepsilon)$-approximate union-closed family ($\ge(1-\varepsilon)$ fraction of pairs $A,B\in\mathcal{F}$ have $A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}$) still has an element in $\ge\tau-\delta$ fraction of sets, $\delta=2\varepsilon(1+\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log|\mathcal{F}|)\to0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$.

**Their proof isolates exactly which of Steps 1–4 above needs exact closure, and it is *only* Step 1 (UB) — and even there, only in a way that degrades continuously.** Concretely (Chase–Lovett §4):
- **The lower bound (LB)-analogue, their Claim 4.1** ($H(A\cup B)\ge\frac{p}{2\varphi}(H(A)+H(B))$, their two-sided version of Step 2–3 combined) is proved **using only** independence of $A,B$ and the marginal bound $\Pr[A_i=0]\ge p$ — **no reference to union-closure of any kind, exact or approximate, appears anywhere in this proof.** It is a fact about *any* two independent random subsets with bounded marginals.
- **Only the upper bound on $H(A\cup B)$** (their proof of Theorem 1.3, second half) uses closure, and there it needs only: $I:=\mathbb{1}[A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}]$ has $\Pr[I=1]\ge1-\varepsilon$. This gives $H(A\cup B)\le H(I)+2\varepsilon\log|\mathcal{F}|+(1+2\varepsilon\text{ overhead})\log|\mathcal{F}|$ instead of the exact $H(A\cup B)\le\log|\mathcal{F}|$ — an $O(\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon))$-additive, $O(\varepsilon)$-relative degradation, nothing more.

**Consequently the entropy method as a whole is provably "closure-agnostic" below the level of $O(\varepsilon)$ corrections** — it never uses any global/structural consequence of *every* pair's union landing in $\mathcal{F}$, only a soft probabilistic version of it. Chase–Lovett's **Example 1.4** (two-layer Boolean-cube construction, $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_1\cup\mathcal{F}_2$ with $\mathcal{F}_1$ a weight-$(\tau n+n^{2/3})$ slice and $\mathcal{F}_2$ the upper half $\{|x|\ge\sigma n\}$, using the identity $2\tau-\tau^2=\sigma$) is only *approximately* union-closed — not exactly — yet already saturates $\tau$. This proves $\tau$ is optimal **for the approximate problem specifically**, and since the entropy method's lower-bound machinery (Claim 4.1 / Step 2–3 above) cannot distinguish "exact" from "$(1-o(1))$-approximate" closure at all, **the method literally cannot certify anything better than $\tau$ for the exact conjecture either, using only the tools it currently has** — any proof that beats $\tau$ for the *exact* Frankl conjecture must use a genuinely combinatorial property of exact closure that has no approximate analogue, i.e. something that would fail to hold (even approximately) for Chase–Lovett's Example 1.4-style near-closed families. No paper in this set identifies what that property might be; this is the clearest statement in the literature of *why* a fundamentally different idea — not a sharper computation within the entropy-method framework — is needed to reach $1/2$.

## 6. One-paragraph roadmap of who did what (chronological, Nov 2022 – Jan 2023)

Gilmer (18 Nov 2022) introduces the entropy method, proves $\Lambda$-type bound with explicit but non-optimal constant $1.26$ at $u=0.01$, giving Frankl's conjecture with constant $0.01$; identifies $\tau=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$ as the exact ceiling of his own method via Examples 1–2, and poses Conjecture 1 (KL route) as a possible way past it. Within days, three independent groups sharpen his Lemma 1 to the exact $\Lambda(u)$: **AHS** (21 Nov, via concavity + Krein–Milman, computer-verified one-variable inequality), **Sawin** (21 Nov, via calculus of variations, symbolic but "complicated" one-variable inequality, plus disproof of Conjecture 1 and an incomplete sketch toward beating $\tau$), **Pebody** (23 Nov, via an $f=H(x)/x$/inverse-function and atom-merging argument, leaving the one-variable inequality itself unproved). **Chase–Lovett** (21 Nov, same day as Sawin) independently reach $\tau$ via a cleaner two-variable-function-minimization route and additionally settle the *approximate* version, proving $\tau$ optimal there too. Ellis (independently) also disproves Gilmer's Conjecture 1. Finally **Boppana** (23 Jan 2023) supplies the missing fully elementary proof of the shared one-variable inequality that AHS needed a computer for, Sawin needed a roundabout argument for, and Pebody left as "to be proven" — closing the last non-elementary gap in the $\tau$-bound's proof.
