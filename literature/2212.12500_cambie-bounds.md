## 0. Abstract (verbatim)

> We improve the best known constant $\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}$ for which the union-closed conjecture is known to be true, by using dependent samples as suggested by Sawin and the entropy approach on this problem initiated by Gilmer. Meanwhile, we focus on the intuition behind this entropy approach and its boundaries.

## 1. Statements: Conjecture, Question, Theorem (verbatim, with paper's numbering)

### Conjecture 1 (Union-closed conjecture)

> *If $\mathcal{F}\not=\{\emptyset\}$ is a union-closed family with ground set $[n],$ then there exists an element $i\in[n]$ such that at least half of the sets in $\mathcal{F}$ contain $i$, i.e., $\lvert\mathcal{F}(i)\rvert\geq\frac{\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}{2}.$*

A union-closed family $\mathcal{F}$ is a collection of sets such that $A,B\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow A\cup B\in\mathcal{F}$, equivalently $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}$ where $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\{A\cup B\colon A,B\in\mathcal{F}\}$. It is *approximate* union-closed if $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}$ holds for almost every choice of $A,B\in\mathcal{F}$.

### Question 2 (Sawin)

> *What is the maximum value $c$ for which there exists an $\alpha\in[0,1]$ such that the following is true? For every $p,q,r$ identically distributed $[0,1]$-valued random variables with expectation less than $c$, where $p$ and $q$ are independent, but $p$ and $r$ are not necessarily independent, we have*
$$
(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]+\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\left[H\left(\max\left(p,r,\min\left(p+r,1/2\right)\right)\right)\right]\geq\,\mathbb{E}[H(p)]. \tag{1}
$$

(The inequality cannot be strict since for any $\{0,1\}$-valued random variable equality holds.)

### Theorem 3 (main theorem — improved union-closed bound)

> *Let $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$ be a nonempty union-closed family. Then there is some element $i\in[n]$ that appears in at least $c\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$ many sets of $\mathcal{F}.$*

where $c \sim 0.3823455$ — precisely $c=0.382345533366703$ (see §2 below), improving the previous best constant $\psi=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}$ (Gilmer / Alweiss–Huang–Sellke / Chase–Lovett / Sawin).

The paper's summary (§1, "So to recap"): *"in this paper, we prove that by taking a linear combination of probability distributions, not all of them being independent, the bound on the union-closed conjecture derived with the entropy approach of Gilmer [12] can be improved slightly."*

### Auxiliary results used in the proof of the exact constant for Question 2

**Lemma 4.** *Assume $p,q$ are independent identically distributed (iid) $[0,1]$-valued random variables with expectation $\mathbb{E}[p]=c\leq 0.39$ such that $\mathbb{P}(p=y)>0$ for some $1/2<y<1$. Then the modified common probability distribution $p',q'$ of $p,q$ for which*
$$\mathbb{P}(p'=1)=\mathbb{P}(p=1)+(2y-1)\mathbb{P}(p=y),\quad \mathbb{P}(p'=y)=0,\quad \mathbb{P}(p'=0.5)=\mathbb{P}(p=0.5)+(2-2y)\mathbb{P}(p=y)$$
*and $\mathbb{P}(p'=y')=\mathbb{P}(p=y')$ for every $y'\in[0,1]\backslash\{0.5,y,1\}$ satisfies*
- *$\mathbb{E}[p']=\mathbb{E}[p]$ and*
- *$w\,\mathbb{E}[H(p')]-\,\mathbb{E}[H(p'+q'-p'q')]>w\,\mathbb{E}[H(p)]-\,\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]$ for every $w\leq 1.044$, where $p',q'$ are iid.*

**Lemma 5.** *Let $p,r$ be identically distributed $[0,1]$-valued random variables, not necessarily independent. Then one can modify the underlying common probability distribution by distributing the probability mass function on $(0.5,1)$ over $0.5$ and $1$ such that $\mathbb{E}[p]$ is the same and*
$$\mathbb{E}\left[H\left(\max\left(p',r',\min\left(p'+r',1/2\right)\right)\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[H\left(\max\left(p,r,\min\left(p+r,1/2\right)\right)\right)\right].$$

**Consequence (end of §3.1):** *if there are probability distributions $p,q$ and $r$ for which Equation 1 is not satisfied for some value of $c$, then these distributions must have support disjoint from $(0.5,1)$.*

**Lemma 6.** *We can assume that $\mathbb{P}(p=r)=1-2a$ and this happens exactly for the $(1-2a)$-quantile $x_{0}$, that is, for every $x<x_{0},$ we have $\mathbb{P}(p=r=x)=\mathbb{P}(p=x)$ and $\mathbb{P}(p=r=x_{0})=1-2a-\mathbb{P}(p<x_{0}).$* (Here $a=\mathbb{P}(p=1)$.)

**Claim 7.** *The function $g\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]\colon x\mapsto h(\min(x,1/2))$ is a concave function. For all $x,x',y,y'\in[0,1/2]$ such that $x<x'$ and $y>y'$, we have*
$$g(x+y)+g(x'+y')\geq g(x+y')+g(x'+y).$$

**Lemma 8.** *The probability distribution $\mu$ [that minimises $H_\mu$ of Eq. (2), below] also minimises*
$$2(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{(p,q)\sim\mu\times\nu}[H(p+q-pq)]-\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\nu}H[p]+\alpha\mathbb{E}'_{p\sim\nu}\left[H\left(\min\left(2p,1/2\right)\right)\right]$$
*among all probability measures $\nu$ for which the $(1-2a)$-quantile is $x_{0}$, $\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\nu}H[p]\leq c$ and $\mathbb{P}_{p\sim\nu}(p=1)=a$.*

## 2. The coupling framework (precise definitions)

### 2.1 Why the entropy approach works (Gilmer's original idea)

Contraposition of Conjecture 1: if every $i\in[n]$ appears in strictly less than half of the sets of $\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}$, then $\mathcal{F}$ is not union-closed, so $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert<\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert$. If one can sample sets from $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$ such that the entropy is strictly larger than $\log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$, then $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert<\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}\rvert$ follows (since a random variable with $N$ outcomes has entropy $\le\log_2N$). It suffices to compare $H(A\cup B)$ with $H(A)$, and for this comparison to work $A$ must be a **uniform** random sample of $\mathcal{F}$. Gilmer's method: take two iid uniform samples $A,B$ from $\mathcal{F}$ and study $H(A\cup B)$.

### 2.2 Sawin's dependent-sampling idea and the precise coupling ("mixture" construction)

Sawin's key generalization is to allow $B$ to be sampled **dependently** (not necessarily independent of $A$), while still requiring each of $A,B$ individually to be marginally uniform over $\mathcal{F}$. The paper works, coordinate-wise, with indicator variables $p=\mathbb{P}(i\in A\mid A_{<i})$ etc. The exact recipe (given fully in §4, "Proof of Theorem 3"):

- $A,B,C$ are three **uniform** samples from $\mathcal{F}$.
- $B$ is sampled **independently** of $A$ and $C$.
- $A$ and $C$ are sampled **element-wise and dependently** with each other.
- For coordinate $i$, given previously-sampled prefixes $A_{<i}=(A_1,\dots,A_{i-1})$ and $C_{<i}=(C_1,\dots,C_{i-1})$ (indicator sequences, $A_j=1_{j\in A}$), define the conditional inclusion fractions
$$f_a=\frac{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: i\in S, S_{<i}=a_{<i}\}\rvert}{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: S_{<i}=a_{<i}\}\rvert},\qquad f_c=\frac{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: i\in S, S_{<i}=c_{<i}\}\rvert}{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: S_{<i}=c_{<i}\}\rvert}.$$
- **Coupling of $(A_i,C_i)$ given the past — the "as negatively correlated as possible" / anti-correlated (Sawin) coupling:**
  - If $\max\{f_a,f_c\}>0.5$: draw $x\sim U([0,1])$ and set $a_i=[x<f_a]$, $c_i=[x<f_c]$. (Both events are anchored at the *same* end $0$, so they overlap maximally — $A_i=1\Rightarrow$ high chance $C_i=1$ too, when both fractions are large. This is the regime relevant to the union bound $\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2))$ saturating at $\min(p+r,1/2)$ once one of $p,r>1/2$.)
  - If $f_a,f_c\leq 1/2$: draw the same $x\sim U([0,1])$ and set $a_i=[x<f_a]$ **but** $c_i=[0.5-f_c<x<0.5]$. This anchors the two events at *opposite* ends of $[0,0.5]$, so that $\{a_i=1\}$ and $\{c_i=1\}$ are disjoint whenever $f_a+f_c\le 0.5$ — i.e. $\mathbb{P}(p=r=1)=0$ in the language of §2.3/§3 (as negatively correlated as possible subject to matching marginals).
  - Consequently, letting $p_i=\mathbb{P}(A_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i})=f_a$ and $r_i=\mathbb{P}(C_{<i+1}\mid C_{<i})=f_c$ be the (matching-marginal, identically distributed) per-coordinate conditional probabilities, one gets exactly
$$\mathbb{P}[(A\cup C)_i \mid A_{<i},C_{<i}]=\max\{p_i,r_i,\min(p_i+r_i,1/2)\}. \tag{explicit union law for the anti-correlated coupling}$$
  - For the independent coupling $(A,B)$: letting $q_i=\mathbb{P}(i\in B\mid B_{<i})$ (independent of $p_i$, identically distributed with it), the standard inclusion–exclusion gives
$$\mathbb{P}[(A\cup B)_i\mid (A\cup B)_{<i}] = p_i+q_i-p_iq_i.$$

- **The linear-combination "mixture" of the two couplings:** the joint law of $(A,B,C)$ (equivalently of the coordinate triple $(p,q,r)$, all identically distributed as some law with $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]<c$) enters Question 2's inequality (1) as a **convex combination with mixture parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$** of the entropy under the independent ($A,B$) coupling and the entropy under the anti-correlated ($A,C$) coupling:
$$(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]+\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))]\ \geq\ \mathbb{E}[H(p)].$$
The optimal numerical value found is $\alpha\sim 0.0356069$ (§3, §4).

- Each of $A,B,C$ is verified to be **exactly** uniformly distributed over $\mathcal{F}$ by direct product-rule telescoping (§4, Eq. S4.Ex21):
$$\mathbb{P}(A=T)=\prod_{i\in T}\frac{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: i\in S, S_{<i}=T_{<i}\}\rvert}{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: S_{<i}=T_{<i}\}\rvert}\cdot\prod_{i\notin T}\frac{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: i\notin S, S_{<i}=T_{<i}\}\rvert}{\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{F}: S_{<i}=T_{<i}\}\rvert}=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}\quad\text{for every }T\in\mathcal{F}.$$

### 2.3 The per-coordinate entropy inequality and chain-rule / "conditioning on the past" argument (§4, Proof of Theorem 3)

Goal: prove
$$(1-\alpha)H(A\cup B)+\alpha H(A\cup C) > H(A) = \log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert.$$

Chain rule (exact, for each coordinate $i$):
$$H((A\cup B)_{<i+1}) = H((A\cup B)_{<i}) + H((A\cup B)_{<i+1}\mid (A\cup B)_{<i}) \ \ge\ H((A\cup B)_{<i}) + H((A\cup B)_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i},B_{<i}),$$
using the **data-processing inequality** ($(A\cup B)_{<i}$ is a deterministic function of $A_{<i},B_{<i}$, so conditioning on the finer $\sigma$-algebra $(A_{<i},B_{<i})$ can only decrease conditional entropy — hence the $\geq$). Analogously
$$H(A_{<i+1}) = H(A_{<i}) + H(A_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i}).$$

So it suffices to prove, telescoping over $i=1,\dots,n$:

1. **Base case ($i=1$):**
$$(1-\alpha)H((A\cup B)_1) + \alpha H((A\cup C)_1) > H(A_1).$$
2. **Inductive step (every $i\ge 2$):**
$$(1-\alpha)H((A\cup B)_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i},B_{<i}) + \alpha H((A\cup C)_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i},C_{<i}) \ \ge\ H(A_{<i+1}\mid A_{<i}).$$

But conditional on the pasts, $(A\cup B)_i$ has the Bernoulli($p_i+q_i-p_iq_i$) conditional law and $(A\cup C)_i$ has the Bernoulli($\max\{p_i,r_i,\min(p_i+r_i,0.5)\}$) conditional law, with $p_i,q_i,r_i$ all having expectation $<c$ by hypothesis (family every element appears in $<c$ fraction of sets $\Rightarrow$ conditional inclusion fractions have expectation $<c$ in the appropriate sense). This is *exactly* Question 2's inequality (1) applied coordinatewise — so both the base case and inductive step follow from the answer to Question 2 with the optimal $(\alpha,c)$ pair. Equality cannot occur at $i=1$, so the base-case inequality is strict, giving overall
$$\max\{H(A\cup C), H(A\cup B)\} > H(A) = \log_2\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert,$$
a contradiction with $A\cup B, A\cup C$ having outcomes only in $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}$ (if every element occurs in $<c\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$ sets) — hence Theorem 3.

## 3. Reduction to a finite-dimensional optimization problem (§3, complete)

### 3.1 Reduction of support to $[0,1/2]\cup\{1\}$ (i.e., excluding $(0.5,1)$)

**Claim (proved via Lemma 4 + Lemma 5):** it suffices to prove Question 2's inequality (1) for $p,q,r$ whose support avoids $(0.5,1)$ entirely.

*Proof sketch, exact mechanism (Lemma 4):* Suppose $\mathbb{P}(p=y)>0$ for some $y\in(1/2,1)$. Redistribute an infinitesimal mass $\varepsilon=\mathbb{P}(p=y)/N$ at a time from $y$ to $\{1,0.5\}$ via the affine split $(2y-1)$ at $1$ and $(2-2y)$ at $0.5$ (this preserves $\mathbb{E}[p]$ exactly, since $(2y-1)\cdot1+(2-2y)\cdot0.5=y$ and the weights sum to $1$). Writing
$$g(x) = (2-2y)h(0.5(1-x)) - h((1-y)(1-x)),$$
one computes (using $h(x+y-xy)=h((1-x)(1-y))$ by symmetry of $h$, and $h(0)=0$):
$$\ln 2 \cdot g'(x) = (y-1)\ln\!\left(\frac{(1-y)(1+x)}{x+y-xy}\right) > 0,\qquad
\ln 2\cdot g''(x) = -\frac{(1-y)(2y-1)}{(x+1)(x+y-xy)} < 0,$$
i.e. $g$ is **increasing and concave**. By Jensen, $\lambda := 2\,\mathbb{E}[g(p)]$ (the first-order change per unit $\varepsilon$ in $\mathbb{E}[H(p'+q'-p'q')]-\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]$) is upper-bounded by $2g(c)$, independent of $\mathbb{P}(p=y)$; also $\mathbb{E}[H(p)]-\mathbb{E}[H(p')] = -g(0)\mathbb{P}(p=y)$. It then suffices to show $2g(c)-g(0)<0$; since $g$ is increasing and $g(0)<0$ (concavity of $h$), it suffices to check $2g(0.39)-1.044\,g(0)<0$ for every $1/2<y<1$ — verified numerically (script: `reduction_UC.sagews` in the paper's GitHub repo). Iterating this redistribution over all mass in $(0.5,1)$ (Lemma 5, using concavity of $g(x)=h(\min(x,1/2))$ and a majorization/rearrangement swap argument) yields the claim; since $1.044>\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$ for the relevant $\alpha$, this argument controls the mixture with weight $(1-\alpha)$ as well.

**Consequence:** any counterexample to Question 2's inequality must have $p,q,r$ supported on $[0,1/2]\cup\{1\}$.

### 3.2 Reduction to support size $\le 3$, and the finite-dimensional optimization problem

Write $a=\mathbb{P}(p=1)\le 1/2$. One first shows WLOG $\mathbb{P}(p=r=1)=0$ (transfer mass between $(1,1)$ and $(x,1),(1,y)$ pairs — a redistribution that strictly decreases $\mathbb{E}[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))]$, using $h(1)=0$). In the remaining regime $p,r\le 1/2$ a.s., so $\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2))=\min(p+r,1/2)$ exactly.

**Lemma 6 (coupling structure on $[0,1/2]$):** letting $x_0$ be the $(1-2a)$-quantile of $p$, the optimal (extremal) coupling of $(p,r)$ on $[0,1/2]$ has $\mathbb{P}(p=r=x)=\mathbb{P}(p=x)$ for every $x<x_0$, and the remaining mass concentrated as $\mathbb{P}(p=r=x_0)=1-2a-\mathbb{P}(p<x_0)$. The proof uses **Claim 7 (Karamata's inequality)**: since $g(x)=h(\min(x,1/2))$ is concave, for $x<x'\le x_0\le y<y'$ [note the paper's exact hypothesis is $x<x'$, $y>y'$ with the "rearrangement" pairing $\{x'+y,x+y'\}$ majorizing $\{x+y,x'+y'\}$], $g(x+y)+g(x'+y')\ge g(x+y')+g(x'+y)$ — i.e. it is never worse to couple *close* quantiles together (concavity favors "assortative"/comonotone coupling on this domain), establishing that $\mathbb{P}(p=r)$ is maximized/optimal exactly on the comonotone-below-$x_0$ coupling.

**The reduced finite-dimensional objective (Eq. 2):** Let $\mu$ be a probability distribution minimizing
$$H_\mu = (1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}_{(p,q)\sim\mu\times\mu}[H(p+q-pq)] + \alpha\,\mathbb{E}'_{p\sim\mu}\left[H(\min(2p,1/2))\right] - \mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mu}[H(p)] \tag{2}$$
among all probability distributions with $\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mu}[H(p)]\le c$ (here $\mathbb{E}'$ denotes the expectation restricted to the $(1-2a)$-quantile mass, per Lemma 6, where $a=\mathbb{P}_{p\sim\mu}(p=1)$). Question 2's inequality (1) is equivalent to $H_\mu\ge 0$ for the minimizer.

**Reduction of Eq. (2) to a single-variable convexity argument (Lemma 8):** for the minimizing $\mu$, and for every fixed $q\in[0,1]$, define
$$F_\mu(q) = \mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mu}\big[\,2(1-\alpha)H(p+q-pq) - h(q)\,\big].$$
It is shown (citing Sawin's Lemma 3) that $\frac{d}{dq}\!\left(q(1-q)\frac{d^2}{dq^2}F_\mu(q)\right)<0$. By direct computation,
$$\ln2\,\frac{d^2}{dq^2}h(2q) = \frac{-2(1-q)}{1-2q},\qquad \frac{d}{dq}\!\left(q(1-q)\ln2\,\frac{d^2}{dq^2}h(2q)\right) = \frac{-2}{(1-2q)^2}<0,$$
so $q(1-q)\ln2\,\frac{d^2}{dq^2}(F_\mu(q)+h(2q))$ is strictly decreasing. Consequently, on
$$I_1=[0,\min\{x_0,1/4\}],\qquad I_2=[\min\{x_0,1/4\},1/2],$$
the functions $F_\mu(q)+h(2q)$ (on $I_1$) and $F_\mu(q)$ (on $I_2$) are each **convex-then-concave** (possibly degenerating to purely convex or purely concave) — i.e. change sign of second derivative at most once, at a critical point $z_1$ (on $I_1$) or $z_2$ (on $I_2$).

**Consequence — support reduction to $\le 4$, then $\le 3$, points:** On an interval where the target function is convex, the minimizing distribution concentrates on **one** point; where convex-then-concave, on **two** points, one of which is the right endpoint of the interval (extended, when relevant, all the way to $1$, since further mass at $1$ only decreases $\mathbb{E}'[H(\min(2p,1/2))]$). This yields **at most 4 support points total** (at most 2 from $I_1$, at most 2 from $I_2$, with $x_0$ (or $1/4$) and possibly $1$ shared/forced). A further argument (redistributing between the boundary points of $I_1$ and $I_2$ when they'd otherwise coincide/duplicate) shows the support has **at most 3 elements with positive probability**, and if exactly 3, at least one of the support points is $\le 1/4$.

### 3.3 The explicit optimization problem in $4$ variables (support $\le 3$)

Once support is reduced to $\{a_1,a_2,1\}$ with probabilities $p_1,p_2,1-p_1-p_2$, Question 2's inequality becomes an explicit **minimization problem in 4 real variables** $(a_1,a_2,p_1,p_2)$ [equivalently $(a_1,a_2,p_1,p_2)$ subject to $p_1,p_2\ge0$, $p_1+p_2\le1$, $a_1,a_2\in[0,1/2]$, plus the expectation constraint $\mathbb{E}[p]\le c$], verified computationally (Maple: minimization in multiple regimes, implicit plots, and plots fixing $a_1$ under $\mathbb{E}[p]=c$). Two local minimizing regions are found: around $a_1=0$ and around $p_1=0$, corresponding to (i) $p$ two-point $\{0,1\}$-valued, and (ii) the atomic 2-point extremal distribution of §2.3/§4 below (support $\{b,1\}$) establishing sharpness.

**The atomic case, worked out explicitly:** with $\mathbb{P}(p=b)=1-a$, $\mathbb{P}(p=1)=a$, $\mathbb{E}[p]=a+(1-a)b\le c$:
$$\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]=(1-a)^2h(2b-b^2),\qquad \mathbb{E}[H(p)]=(1-a)h(b),$$
$$\mathbb{E}\left[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))\right]\ge(1-2a)\,h(\min(2b,1/2)).$$
The (worst case $\mathbb{P}(p=r=1)=0$) inequality to verify is
$$(1-\alpha)(1-a)^2h(2b-b^2) + \alpha(1-2a)h(\min(2b,1/2)) - (1-a)h(b) \ge 0,$$
equivalently, expanding as a quadratic in $a$ (fixed $b$):
$$(1-\alpha)h(2b-b^2)\,a^2 + \big(-2(1-\alpha)h(2b-b^2) - 2\alpha h(\min(2b,1/2)) + h(b)\big)a + O_{b,\alpha}(1) \ge 0.$$
This has positive leading coefficient and attains its minimum at
$$a = 1 + \frac{2\alpha h(\min(2b,1/2)) - h(b)}{2(1-\alpha)h(2b-b^2)} > \frac{c-b}{1-b},$$
so it suffices to verify at $a=\frac{c-b}{1-b}$ (i.e. at $\mathbb{E}[p]=c$ exactly, the boundary case) — script `Sharpness.sagews`.

### 3.4 Precise verification: reduction to a $2$-variable minimization

Combining §3.1–§3.2 with the framework of Yu [22], the joint distribution of $(p,r)$ reduces to exactly one of two forms:
- $p=r$, support of $p$ of size $\le2$ (elements $\le1/2$), **or**
- support $\{a_1,a_2,1\}$ with $p_1=1-2p_2$, $\mathbb{P}(p=a_2,r=1)=\mathbb{P}(p=1,r=a_2)=p_2$.

Equivalently, in Yu's notation, the joint distribution $P_{pr}$ has the form
$$(1-\beta)Q_{a,a}+\beta Q_{b,b}\qquad\text{or}\qquad (1-\beta)Q_{a,a}+\beta Q_{1,b}\qquad(a\le b),$$
where, for fixed $c$: in the first case $a<c<b$ and $\beta=\frac{c-a}{b-a}$; in the second case $\beta=\frac{2(c-a)}{1+b-2a}$. This reduces the **entire verification of Question 2 to an inequality in exactly two variables** $(a,b)$, solved numerically with graphical confirmation of global-vs-local-minimum behavior (documents `FinalComputation23`, `FinalComputation24` in the paper's GitHub repository `https://github.com/StijnCambie/UCconjecture`). If $p=r$ the inequality is strict; in the case with support containing $1$, the atomic distribution of §2.3 is the **unique** minimizer and the inequality is tight.

## 4. Sharpness: the extremal distribution and why it defeats the mixture coupling (§2.3, verbatim reproduction — the central result for (c))

This is stated in §2.3 ("Upper bound for Question 2 and limits on the approach of Sawin"), and it is this construction that proves $c=0.382345533366703$ is the **exact** answer to Question 2 (i.e. Sawin's mixture-coupling method cannot certify any constant larger than this, for *any* choice of $\alpha$).

### 4.1 Setup: numerical constants $b_1, b_2, b, a$

Let $h(x)=-x\log_2(x)-(1-x)\log_2(1-x)$ (binary entropy, base 2). Let the roots of
$$h(x)(2-h(x)) - h(2x-x^2)$$
be $0<b_1<b_2<1$. Then
$$b_1\sim 0.139499451909862,\qquad b_2\sim 0.329454738503037.$$
Choose
$$b=b_2,\qquad a=\frac{1-h(b)}{2-h(b)}\sim 0.0788772927059232.$$

### 4.2 The extremal 2-point distribution and its "as negatively correlated as possible" coupling

Let $p,q,r$ be three identically distributed $[0,1]$-valued random variables with **exactly 2-point support** $\{1,b\}$:
$$\mathbb{P}(p=1)=a,\qquad \mathbb{P}(p=b)=1-a,$$
such that:
- $p$ and $q$ are **independent** (matching Question 2's requirement), and
- $p$ and $r$ are coupled to be **as negatively correlated as possible** in the precise sense
$$\mathbb{P}(p=r=1)=0.$$
(Given the marginal $\{a\text{ at }1,\ 1-a\text{ at }b\}$ with $a\le1/2$, this is realized, e.g., by the anti-correlated/"disjoint" coupling described in §2.2 above: the event $\{p=1\}$ and $\{r=1\}$ are made disjoint, so whenever one of $p,r$ equals $1$ the other equals $b$; the only remaining freedom is how the "$b,b$" mass is arranged, which does not affect the value of $\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2))$ since $b<1/2$.)

For this choice, one computes:
$$\mathbb{E}[p] = 0.382345533366703 \tag{$=c$, the claimed optimal constant}$$
and the identity (numerically verified; script `Sharpness.sagews`)
$$(1-a)^2 h(2b-b^2) = (1-2a) = (1-a)h(b),$$
which is **equivalent to**
$$\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\left[H\left(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2))\right)\right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}[H(p)]. \tag{sharpness identity}$$

### 4.3 Why this distribution defeats/saturates the mixture coupling — the exact mechanism

Because **both** terms on the left of Question 2's inequality (1) — the independent-coupling entropy $\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)]$ *and* the anti-correlated-coupling entropy $\mathbb{E}[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))]$ — are **simultaneously and exactly equal** to $\mathbb{E}[H(p)]$ for this single 2-point distribution, *any* convex combination
$$(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)] + \alpha\,\mathbb{E}[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))]$$
also equals $\mathbb{E}[H(p)]$ **exactly**, for **every** $\alpha\in[0,1]$. So the inequality in (1) is tight (equality) at this distribution regardless of how one mixes the two coupling strategies — there is no slack for a linear combination to exploit. This is exactly why $c = \mathbb{E}[p] = 0.382345533366703$ is a **hard ceiling** for Sawin's method: no linear combination $(1-\alpha)$-independent-plus-$\alpha$-anticorrelated can certify a larger $c$, since this single family of distributions (varying $b$ near $b_2$, $a=\frac{1-h(b)}{2-h(b)}$) saturates *both* terms simultaneously.

The paper states the conclusion explicitly (§2.3, penultimate paragraph):

> *"Hence no linear combination satisfies Equation 1 with a strict inequality and local perturbations (increasing $a$) will result in counterexamples when $c$ is allowed to be slightly larger."*

and:

> *"From these observations, one can conclude that one cannot aim to prove the result with a constant better than $0.382345533366703$ with the exact suggested approach of Sawin."*

### 4.4 A single-term (non-mixed) argument is insufficient — a second explicit witness

To show that even taking $\alpha=0$ (i.e., using only the anti-correlated term $\mathbb{E}[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))]$ alone, without any linear combination with the independent term) cannot reach $\psi=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx 0.38197$, the paper exhibits: for $p,r$ i.d. with $\mathbb{P}(p=r=1)=0$, $\mathbb{P}(p=b)=1-a$, $\mathbb{P}(p=1)=a$, where $b=\frac14$ and $a=\frac{1-h(b)}{2-h(b)}+\epsilon$ for small $\epsilon>0$:
$$\mathbb{E}\left[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))\right] < \mathbb{E}[H(p)],$$
with $\mathbb{E}[p] < 0.37 < \frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$ — i.e. considering the single anti-correlated term alone would not even reproduce Gilmer's original bound $\psi$, let alone improve it. **This is why the linear combination (mixture with $\alpha$) in Question 2 is "somewhat necessary"** — neither pure coupling strategy alone suffices; only the specific mixture, optimized at $\alpha\sim0.0356069$, reaches (and exactly saturates, at the 2-point distribution of §4.2) the constant $c=0.382345533366703$.

## 5. Optimal mixture parameter $\alpha$ and how it is obtained (§3, opening)

Optimal $\alpha\sim 0.0356069$ is derived by comparing derivatives of the atomic (2-point, support $\{x,1\}$) solutions with expectation $c$, **around $x=b$** (the value from §4.1). Precisely, define
$$g_1(x) = \mathbb{E}[H(p+q-pq)] - \mathbb{E}[H(p)] = \mathbb{P}(p=x)^2\,h(2x-x^2) - \mathbb{P}(p=x)\,h(x),$$
$$g_2(x) = \mathbb{E}\left[H(\max(p,r,\min(p+r,1/2)))\right] - \mathbb{E}[H(p)] = \big(1-2\,\mathbb{P}(p=1)\big) - \mathbb{P}(p=x)\,h(x),$$
where $\mathbb{P}(p=1)=\frac{c-x}{1-x}$, $\mathbb{P}(p=x)=1-\frac{c-x}{1-x}$ (i.e. $p$ supported on $\{x,1\}$ with expectation exactly $c$). Question 2's inequality (1) is then equivalent to
$$(1-\alpha)g_1(x) + \alpha g_2(x) \ge 0,$$
and for this to have a critical (tangency) point exactly at $x=b$ (matching the 2-point extremal distribution of §4.2), $\alpha$ must satisfy
$$(1-\alpha)g_1'(b) + \alpha g_2'(b) = 0 \iff \alpha = \frac{g_1'(b)}{g_1'(b)-g_2'(b)}.$$
Numerically this gives $\alpha\sim 0.0356069$.

## 6. All other explicit inequalities / calculus lemmas used

1. **Jensen's inequality** for the concave function $g(x)=(2-2y)h(0.5(1-x))-h((1-y)(1-x))$ (§3.1, proof of Lemma 4): $\lambda=2\,\mathbb{E}[g(p)]\le 2g(c)$.
2. **Karamata's inequality** (cited as reference [14]) applied to the concave function $g(x)=h(\min(x,1/2))$ and the majorization $\{x'+y,x+y'\}\succ\{x+y,x'+y'\}$ (Claim 7, §3.2).
3. **Data-processing inequality** for conditional entropy: $H(Y\mid X)\ge H(Y\mid Z)$ when $X$ is a deterministic function of $Z$ (used implicitly in §4's chain-rule step, replacing conditioning on $(A\cup B)_{<i}$ by conditioning on the finer $(A_{<i},B_{<i})$).
4. **Chain rule for entropy:** $H(X_{<i+1}) = H(X_{<i}) + H(X_{<i+1}\mid X_{<i})$, applied telescopically over $i=1,\dots,n$ (§4).
5. **Convexity/concavity computations for $F_\mu(q)+h(2q)$ / $F_\mu(q)$ (§3.2, Lemma 8's proof):**
$$\ln2\,\frac{d^2}{dq^2}h(2q) = \frac{-2(1-q)}{1-2q},\qquad \frac{d}{dq}\Big(q(1-q)\ln2\,\frac{d^2}{dq^2}h(2q)\Big)=\frac{-2}{(1-2q)^2}<0.$$
6. **Identity used repeatedly:** $h(x+y-xy) = h((1-x)(1-y))$ (symmetry of $h$ about $1/2$, since $1-(x+y-xy)=(1-x)(1-y)$).
7. **Numerical roots** of $h(x)(2-h(x))-h(2x-x^2)$: $b_1\sim0.139499451909862$, $b_2\sim0.329454738503037$ (§2.3).
8. **Threshold constant $1.044$** in Lemma 4's statement, used because $1.044 > \frac{1}{1-\alpha}$ for the optimal $\alpha\sim0.0356069$ (§3.1, final paragraph before Lemma 5's use), ensuring the support-reduction argument controls the $(1-\alpha)$-weighted term of the mixture as well.

## 7. Reduction of support size — summary of the argument for $\le 3$ points

Two independent supporting facts are used together:

(i) **§3.1 (Lemmas 4–5):** it suffices to consider $p,q,r$ with support avoiding $(0.5,1)$ — any mass there can be redistributed onto $\{0.5,1\}$ (preserving the mean) while only *helping* (not increasing the LHS below the RHS) in Question 2's inequality, by a first-order (Jensen/concavity) redistribution argument that also controls the independent-coupling term via the $1.044$ threshold.

(ii) **§3.2 (Lemma 6, Claim 7 / Karamata, Lemma 8):** on the remaining domain $[0,1/2]\cup\{1\}$, the anti-correlated coupling is optimized by a comonotone-below-quantile structure (Lemma 6), and the reduced 1-variable functional $F_\mu(q)$ (together with $F_\mu(q)+h(2q)$ on the low sub-interval) has second derivative changing sign **at most once** on each of $I_1=[0,\min\{x_0,1/4\}]$ and $I_2=[\min\{x_0,1/4\},1/2]$ — a convex-then-concave shape. This forces the minimizing $\mu$ to be supported on at most 2 points per sub-interval (one of which is typically the interval's right endpoint, extendable to $1$), giving $\le4$ points overall; a further boundary-matching argument (§3.2, final paragraphs) drops this to **at most 3** points, with at least one of them $\le1/4$ when exactly 3 are used.

Combined with Yu's [22] framework (§3.4), the joint law of $(p,r)$ reduces further to depend on only **2 real parameters** $(a,b)$ (with $\beta$ determined by $a,b,c$), giving the final, computer-verified 2-variable minimization.

## 8. Barriers, remarks on limits, and next steps (§2.2–2.4, author's own discussion)

### 8.1 Why the entropy method (even with dependent sampling) has a ceiling

- The naive "large uniform density" family
$$\mathcal{F} = \{A\subset[n] : \lvert A\rvert = \psi n + g(n) \ \vee\ \lvert A\rvert \ge (1-\psi)n\} = \binom{[n]}{\psi n+g(n)} \cup \binom{[n]}{\ge(1-\psi)n}$$
(with $\psi=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$ the smallest root of $x^2-3x+1=0$, and $g(n)$ both $o(n)$ and $\omega(n^{0.5})$, e.g. $g(n)\sim n^{2/3}$ as in [7]) is *approximate* union-closed, and under **independent** uniform sampling $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}=\binom{[n]}{\ge\psi n+g(n)}$ — only a vanishing fraction of $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}$ lies in $\mathcal{F}$, which naively suggests $\psi$ is a hard ceiling for *any* entropy argument. **The paper's key observation (§2.2) is that this naive conclusion is wrong**: with Sawin's dependency, the union of two sets from this same family will almost surely have size $(0.5+o(1))n$ instead — i.e. the *dependent*-coupling entropy calculation behaves completely differently from the independent one on this example, which is precisely why room exists to beat $\psi$.
- Nonetheless (§2.3), the paper proves a genuine, non-heuristic ceiling for Sawin's *specific* method: the exact 2-point distribution of §4.2 above shows that **no** choice of $\alpha\in[0,1]$ in the linear combination (1) can certify a constant above $c=0.382345533366703$. This is a rigorous matching upper bound for Question 2, making the answer to Question 2 *exactly* $c$ (not just $\ge c$).
- A single (non-mixed) use of the anti-correlated coupling alone (§2.3, second example, $b=1/4$) cannot even reach $\psi$ — underscoring that the linear combination is "somewhat necessary" (footnote 2: *"variants will not be simpler"*) for the method to work at all.

### 8.2 What would be needed to go beyond $c\approx0.38234$

- The author states explicitly (§1, end) that since the resulting constant is **not $\frac12$**, "another core method will be needed for the full resolution" of the union-closed conjecture — i.e. Sawin's exact linear-combination coupling, however precisely optimized, cannot itself resolve the conjecture (which needs $c=1/2$); it can only push the known constant up from $\psi\approx0.38197$ to $c\approx0.38235$.
- Consequently, "an even more rigorous analysis than has been done is unnecessary, as it does not contribute to further understanding the underlying principles" (§1) — the paper deliberately does *not* pursue higher-precision computer verification of the $c\approx0.38234$ constant beyond what is needed, because doing so would not illuminate a path past the barrier.
- Reference is made to Liu [17] ("Improving the Lower Bound for the Union-closed Sets Conjecture via Conditionally IID Coupling," arXiv:2306.08824), which gives "a variant with tiny improvement" (footnote 3) using a **9-variable** optimization (vs. this paper's reduced 2-variable one, or the initial 4-variable one) — indicating that further generalizing the coupling family yields only marginal gains at much higher technical cost, consistent with the idea that Sawin-type couplings are close to fundamentally exhausted for this approach.
- Yu [22] ("Dimension-free bounds for the union-closed sets conjecture," Entropy 25 (2023)(5)) is cited as giving the general optimization framework this paper specializes and sharpens (from Yu's 4-variable version down to a 2-variable one in §3.4); the paper's §3.4 addition (prompted by a referee) explicitly ties the two approaches together, showing they characterize the same extremal structure.
- No explicit new technique for surpassing this class of couplings is proposed in the paper; the stated position is that this constant is the **exact** ceiling of Sawin's coupling idea, and a qualitatively different method is required to approach $c=1/2$.
